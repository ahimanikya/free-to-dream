# Local voices, shared joy

One poem, many musical settings. Every version should express calm, soulful joy, affection, wonder and the freedom to dream. Cultural character should come through in vocal phrasing and melody as well as instrumentation.

## What the musical audit changed

The audit covered all 100 language pages. The 93 entries in the Indian collection now have individual vocal and melodic directions, with locally chosen rhythms instead of a compulsory 6/8 arrangement. The seven recently revised international settings are retained. “Indian collection” describes this project's grouping; many of its languages and musical traditions cross national borders.

The audit preserved all lyrics, titles, source poems, meaning guides and translation-review statuses. It reviewed written musical briefs and reference material. It did not listen to or certify recordings, nor did it complete the pending native-language adaptations.

## Shared feeling, individual music

- Let a fluent reading establish word boundaries, phrasing and natural breaths before fixing the tune.
- Choose a specific musical setting suited to the poem. A language can support many traditions and contemporary styles.
- Give local melody and vocal delivery a clear role. Guitar, flute, strings and harmony are choices, not a required collection-wide ensemble.
- Keep an unhurried pulse. A six-beat tala and Western 6/8 notation are not interchangeable instructions; a musician should establish the actual rhythmic treatment.
- Leave a distinct breath between repeated gathering phrases and a fuller pause after the potter declaration. Soft instruments can sustain beneath both.
- Add warmth through the verses without accelerating. Finish with peaceful, hopeful contentment.

## What the references establish

Each language page identifies the role of its reference. An instrument catalogue can support an instrument's association with a community. A festival report can document a performance. A language dictionary can help an adapter. None of these alone verifies a newly generated melody, vocal accent or traditional rhythmic pattern.

For pages with only community or language context, the arrangement is a contemporary proposal to develop with a local singer. All new musical treatments still need musician review, including those with good written references.

Several reference choices were repaired or narrowed: Sambalpuri now uses Sambalpur district's cultural account; Marathi uses a direct NCPA bhavgeet programme; Kutchi uses the Living Lightly project; Kokborok uses Tripura University's instrument references. Unsupported Khortha-specific Jhumar wording was removed. Sanskrit retains a spacious chamber character with luminous warmth, and Telugu retains its preferred acoustic intimacy.

## Collaborator review

Start with the meaning guide and the language-specific review on each page. For a pending adaptation, establish the intended variety, script and complete lyric first. Then record a natural spoken reading and work out a melody that keeps its meaning audible. A local musician should identify any traditional influence precisely and help choose a suitable pulse.

When suggesting a change, name the language and variety, quote the line or prompt sentence, propose a replacement and explain the reason. For a recording, include its timestamp and the exact audible issue. Add a reference or a short demonstration you are entitled to share when it helps. Keep text review and performance review separate.
